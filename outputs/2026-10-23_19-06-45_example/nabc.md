# NABC summary: water-based lignin binder for lithium-ion battery cathodes

*Source: invention disclosure dated 2026-09-15 (confidential, unpublished). No web searches were made. Comments marked **[Advisor note]** come from general industry knowledge, not the disclosure, and should be checked.*

**Summary:** A chemically modified kraft lignin binder dissolves in water and replaces PVDF in nickel-rich (NMC811) cathodes, so cathode coating no longer needs the toxic, costly solvent NMP.

---

## Need
- **Who has the problem:** Lithium-ion cell makers that coat cathodes. Today they almost always use PVDF binder dissolved in NMP. NMP is toxic and expensive, and it has to be recovered in drying ovens that use a lot of energy.
- **How important:** The disclosure does not quantify this (no cost share of binder or solvent, no figures for drying energy or CAPEX). **[Advisor note]** PVDF and NMP face cost, health and safety, and regulatory pressure (NMP restrictions, possible PFAS restrictions on fluoropolymers in the EU). This could make the need urgent, but we need customer evidence.
- Battery makers have not been contacted, so **customer validation is Not stated.**

## Approach
- **Solution:** Kraft lignin, a by-product of pulp mills, is modified so it dissolves in water and binds strongly to NMC811 particles. The slurry is water-based, so no NMP is needed. The chemistry of the modification is Not stated.
- **How it meets the need:** Cell makers would not need NMP or its recovery. Drying could run at lower temperature, and the feedstock is cheap and renewable.
- **Likely route to market:**
  - *Licence to a binder or chemicals supplier, or to a pulp company*: probably the most realistic route. A Finnish pulp company has shown informal interest in supplying lignin. It could become the producer and licensee.
  - *Licence to a cell maker*: possible but slow, because qualification cycles are long.
  - *Spin-off*: possible because the postdoc wants to continue, but there is no business lead yet (see Team).
- **IP status:** Nothing has been published yet, but a manuscript is planned for spring 2027. **A patent application must be filed before then.** Novelty and freedom to operate are Not stated.

## Benefits per costs
| Benefit | Evidence in disclosure | Numbers |
|---|---|---|
| Removes NMP (cost, toxicity, solvent recovery) | Water-based slurry demonstrated | Savings not quantified |
| Lower drying energy | Water evaporates at a lower temperature than NMP | Not stated |
| Cheaper binder material | "Lignin costs a fraction of PVDF" | Price per kg Not stated |
| Equal or better cycle life | Coin cells, 500 cycles | 91% capacity retention vs 89% for PVDF (same lab) |
| Coating adhesion | Peel strength "similar to PVDF" | Values Not stated |
| Bio-based and fluorine-free | Implied | Not stated |

- **Costs to the customer:** These are Not stated. They could include changes to the coating and drying line, qualification and validation costs, and the price of the modified binder (modification cost and yield are Not stated).
- **Caveat:** The 2-percentage-point gain in capacity retention comes from coin cells in one lab. The number of cells and the spread in results are Not stated, so we should not rely on it as a selling point yet.

## Competition
- **Current:** PVDF in NMP, the dominant standard.
- **Alternatives:** The disclosure does not discuss competing technologies. **[Advisor note]** Likely competitors include:
  - other aqueous cathode binders (e.g. CMC, alginate, polyacrylic-acid and acrylic systems, other lignin- or bio-based binders under academic study)
  - dry-electrode processing, which needs no solvent at all
  - fluorine-free binders processed in non-NMP solvents
- **Why this may be better:** The feedstock is cheap and abundant, the binder is bio-based and fluorine-free, it shows cycling performance comparable to PVDF, and a pulp company could provide the supply chain. A real competitive analysis and patent landscape are needed. Differentiation from other aqueous and lignin binders is **Not stated**.

## Readiness and funding
- **Development stage:** Lab scale only (coin cells, 50 g binder batches). **[Advisor note]** This is about TRL 3–4. The next steps are pouch or larger cells, testing on a pilot coating line, and kg-scale binder synthesis.
- **Past funding:** Business Finland Research to Business pre-study (ended 2026) and an Academy of Finland project.
- **Possible next funding:** Business Finland Research to Business full project (a natural next step after the pre-study), Aalto proof-of-concept or internal commercialisation funds, and co-funded development with the pulp company. Later options include EU programmes such as EIC Transition or Batteries Europe/Horizon calls. Fit and timing still need to be checked.

## Assessment
**Main strengths**
- It addresses a real pain point (NMP/PVDF) where regulation is also pushing for change.
- Early performance matches PVDF.
- Cheap, renewable feedstock, and a supply partner has shown interest.
- Nothing has been published yet, so patent protection is still possible.
- There is a motivated postdoc.

**Main risks**
- **Disclosure timing:** a publication is planned for spring 2027 and no patent has been filed yet.
- **Technical:** **[Advisor note]** Nickel-rich NMC811 is known to be sensitive to water processing (lithium leaching, high slurry pH, aluminium foil corrosion). Long-term stability, high-voltage behaviour and performance at scale are unproven.
- Lignin varies from batch to batch, which threatens reproducibility.
- There is no contact with battery makers and no market validation. Qualification cycles are long.
- No business lead. Competition from other aqueous binders and from dry-electrode processing.

**Questions for the researchers**
1. What is new about the modification chemistry compared with published lignin and aqueous binders? Can we file a patent application well before the spring 2027 manuscript?
2. How do you deal with NMC811's sensitivity to water (pH, lithium leaching, aluminium corrosion)? Do you have data beyond coin cells, for example at high loading, in pouch cells, or at higher voltage?
3. How reproducible are the results across different lignin batches and sources? What would kg-scale production cost per kg compared with PVDF?
4. Which customer do you picture first (binder supplier, pulp company or cell maker)? What would the informal pulp-company contact commit to?
5. Postdoc: would you lead a spin-off, and would you be willing to bring in a business co-founder? What do you need over the next 12–18 months?
