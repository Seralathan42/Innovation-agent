# NABC summary: Water-based lignin binder for lithium-ion battery cathodes

**Inventors:** Dr. A. Example (postdoc) and Prof. B. Example, Dept. of Chemical and Metallurgical Engineering · **Disclosure date:** 2026-09-15 · *Fictional test example*

**In one sentence:** This is a chemically modified kraft lignin, a pulp-mill by-product, that dissolves in water and replaces PVDF as the cathode binder. Cathode slurries could then be made without the toxic solvent NMP, and lab cells perform as well as with PVDF.

---

## Need
- **Who has the problem:** Makers of lithium-ion battery cells and cathodes. The disclosure says PVDF is "almost always" the cathode binder.
- **The problem:** PVDF is a fluorinated polymer and must be dissolved in NMP. NMP is toxic and expensive, and it has to be recovered in drying ovens that use a lot of energy.
- **How important it is:** The disclosure gives no figures for the cost, energy or regulatory burden. *General context, not from the disclosure:* the industry has strong reasons to get rid of NMP. These include worker-safety and chemicals regulation (especially in the EU), pressure on fluorinated polymers (PFAS), and the energy needed for drying and solvent recovery. Before the meeting, check how strongly cell makers feel this pain and how much they would pay to remove it.

## Approach
- **Solution:** Kraft lignin is chemically modified so that it dissolves in water and binds strongly to nickel-rich NMC811 cathode particles. The slurry is water-based, so no NMP is needed and drying happens at a lower temperature.
- **How it meets the need:** It removes the solvent and the fluorinated polymer in one step, and it uses a cheap, renewable raw material that is already available in large volumes.
- **Likely route to market:**
  1. **Licence** to a binder or specialty-chemicals supplier, or to a pulp company that wants to sell higher-value lignin products. A Finnish pulp company has shown informal interest.
  2. **Spin-off** built around the postdoc, who wants to continue. This only works if business leadership is added.
  3. Selling the technology directly to a battery maker is less likely. Cell makers usually buy binders from suppliers rather than make them.
- The modification chemistry, the method and the use in cathodes should be checked for patentability (see Risks).

## Benefits per costs
| Item | Disclosure says | Numbers |
|---|---|---|
| Cycle life | 91% capacity kept after 500 cycles vs 89% for PVDF (coin cells, own lab) | Slightly better than PVDF, not worse |
| Coating adhesion | Peel strength similar to PVDF | Not stated |
| Binder material cost | Lignin costs "a fraction" of PVDF | Not stated |
| Cost of the modified binder at scale | — | Not stated |
| Drying energy | Lower, because water evaporates at a lower temperature than NMP | Not stated |
| NMP purchase and recovery avoided | Implied | Not stated |
| Switching costs for the customer (line changes, qualification) | — | Not stated |

**Overall:** The value case is believable in direction: no NMP, a cheaper raw material, less drying energy, and performance at least equal to PVDF. However, none of the cost savings are put into numbers. The cost of producing the *modified* lignin is not given, and this is what matters, not the cost of raw lignin.

## Competition
- **Current standard:** PVDF in NMP. It is established, proven and fully qualified in production lines.
- **Alternatives and likely competitors** (general knowledge, not from the disclosure):
  - Better NMP recovery or substitute solvents, which keep PVDF but reduce the solvent problem.
  - Other water-based cathode binders, such as polymers like those already used water-based on the anode side, polyacrylates, and other bio-based polymers.
  - Dry electrode processing, which needs no solvent at all and is being developed by large players.
  - Other academic groups working on lignin-based binders. This affects both competition and prior art.
- **Why this could be better:** The raw material is cheap, renewable and fluorine-free. It has been shown on nickel-rich NMC811, which is the harder and commercially relevant chemistry, and cycling matches PVDF. The disclosure does not compare it with other water-based or dry-process alternatives.

## Team
- **Dr. A. Example (postdoc):** wants to continue with the technology, so a possible founder or technical lead.
- **Prof. B. Example:** wants to stay in academia, so a possible scientific advisor.
- **Missing:** business and commercial leadership, contacts with battery makers, experience scaling up chemical processes, and industrial know-how in battery qualification. This is the most critical gap at the moment.

## Readiness and funding
- **Readiness:** Lab scale only, with coin cells and 50 g binder batches. This is roughly TRL 3–4 (our estimate; the disclosure gives no TRL). It has not been tested in pouch or larger cells, on pilot coating lines, or in long-term or real-world conditions.
- **Earlier funding:** a Business Finland Research to Business (R2B) pre-study (ended 2026) and an Academy of Finland project.
- **Possible next funding:** Business Finland Research to Business main phase, which would follow naturally from the pre-study. Later, EIC Transition or a co-development project with the pulp company and a battery or binder partner. Aalto Innovation proof-of-concept support could pay for the patent landscape review and first market interviews.
- **Publication:** No prior publications. A manuscript is planned for spring 2027, so **a patent application must be filed before submission.**

## Assessment
**Main strengths**
- It addresses a clear industry pain point: getting rid of NMP and PVDF.
- Performance in lab cells matches PVDF on a commercially relevant chemistry (NMC811).
- The raw material is cheap and abundant, and fits Finland's forest and battery strengths.
- A raw-material partner has already shown interest, and a motivated postdoc wants to carry it forward.
- It is still unpublished, so a patent is still possible.

**Main risks**
- **Technical:** Nickel-rich cathodes like NMC811 are generally known to be sensitive to water processing (lithium leaching, higher slurry pH, corrosion of the aluminium foil). The disclosure does not address this. The results are also small and from a single lab.
- **IP:** Lignin binders are an active research topic, so there may be prior art. Freedom to operate is unknown.
- **Market:** There has been no contact with battery makers. Qualifying a new material in cell production takes several years and the industry is conservative. Dry electrode processing could make all solvent-based binders less relevant.
- **Economics:** The cost of the modification step and the consistency of lignin from batch to batch are unknown.
- **Team:** There is no business lead, and the professor will not commit.

**Questions to ask the researchers**
1. How do you deal with the moisture sensitivity of NMC811 in a water-based slurry (lithium leaching, pH, aluminium foil corrosion)? How many cells and batches are the 91% vs 89% results based on?
2. What exactly is new in the modification chemistry compared with published lignin binder work? Have you done any prior-art search, and can we file before the spring 2027 manuscript?
3. What would the modified binder cost per kg at scale? How consistent is kraft lignin between mills and batches, and how does that affect performance?
4. What is the plan and timeline for scale-up (larger batches, pouch cells, pilot coating line), and which industrial partners could provide access?
5. Does the postdoc want to found a company or move into a licensing partnership? What is the status of the pulp company's interest, and could they co-fund the next phase?
