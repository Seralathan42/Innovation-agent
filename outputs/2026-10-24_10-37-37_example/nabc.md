# NABC summary: water-based lignin binder for lithium-ion battery cathodes

*Disclosure dated 2026-09-15 (marked as a fictional test example). Confidential. No web searches were run. Points that come from general field knowledge rather than the disclosure are marked "[advisor note]" and should be checked.*

**Summary:** A chemically modified kraft lignin, a by-product of pulp mills, that dissolves in water and binds nickel-rich cathode material (NMC811). It could replace PVDF binder and the toxic NMP solvent in cathode manufacturing.

---

## Need
- **Who has the problem:** Cathode and battery cell manufacturers. Today they coat cathodes with PVDF, which has to be dissolved in NMP. The disclosure calls NMP toxic and expensive, and says it must be recovered in energy-intensive drying ovens.
- **How important it is:** Not quantified in the disclosure. The cost of NMP, the energy used for drying and solvent recovery, and the share these take of cell production cost are all Not stated.
- **[advisor note]** Other pressures may add to the need: NMP is restricted in the EU (REACH), PVDF is a fluoropolymer and could be caught by the proposed EU PFAS restriction, and the industry wants to cut CO₂ per kWh. If confirmed, these would make the need more urgent and help the sales pitch.

## Approach
- **Solution:** Kraft lignin is chemically modified so that it dissolves in water and binds strongly to NMC811 particles. The cathode slurry is then water-based and needs no NMP.
- **How it meets the need:** It removes the toxic solvent and the solvent-recovery step. It lowers drying temperature and energy, and it swaps a costly fluoropolymer for a cheap by-product. The modification chemistry is not described in the disclosure.
- **Likely route to market:** Not stated. Options:
  1. **Licence or sale to a binder or chemicals supplier, or to a pulp company.** A Finnish pulp company has shown informal interest in supplying lignin, and it could become a licensee rather than just a supplier.
  2. **Licence to a cell or cathode maker.** This is harder, because battery qualification cycles are long.
  3. **Spin-off.** The postdoc wants to continue, but there is no business person on the team yet.

  A licence to an established materials or pulp player looks the most realistic at this stage.

## Benefits per costs
| Benefit to the customer | Evidence in the disclosure | Numbers |
|---|---|---|
| Lower binder material cost | "Lignin costs a fraction of PVDF" | Not stated |
| No NMP purchase or recovery | Slurry is water-based | Not stated |
| Lower drying energy | Water evaporates at a lower temperature than NMP | Not stated |
| Same or better performance | 91% capacity kept after 500 cycles vs 89% for PVDF (coin cells, same lab); similar peel strength | Number of cells, error bars and test conditions Not stated |

- **Costs to the customer:** Not stated. These could include re-qualifying the cell, changing slurry and coating processes, the cost of the modification chemistry itself, and possibly managing moisture.
- **[advisor note]** Water-based processing of NMC811 is known to be hard. Lithium leaches out of the particles, the slurry pH rises, the aluminium foil can corrode, and moisture left in the electrode can hurt performance. Any extra process steps needed to deal with this would eat into the benefit.

## Competition
- **Current standard:** PVDF in NMP, which the disclosure says is used almost universally.
- **[advisor note] Other alternatives not covered in the disclosure:**
  - Other water-based cathode binders, such as CMC/SBR-type, acrylic and alginate binders. Water-based CMC/SBR is already standard for anodes.
  - Fluorine-free binders from large chemical companies.
  - Dry-electrode processing, which uses no solvent at all and is being pursued by major cell makers.
  - Other academic work on lignin binders.
- **Why this could be better:** It is cheap, bio-based and comes from a by-product. It has a supply partner in Finland. Lab results match or slightly beat PVDF on NMC811, the hardest cathode chemistry to process in water.
- **Prior art or patent search:** Not stated. A search is needed before filing.

## Readiness and funding
- **Development stage:** Lab scale only: coin cells and 50 g binder batches. Roughly TRL 3–4 [advisor estimate]. Pouch cells, larger batches, testing on a pilot coating line and long-term or high-temperature cycling have not been done.
- **IP:** Nothing has been published yet, but a journal manuscript is planned for spring 2027. The patent position needs to be settled (assessed and, if worthwhile, filed) before then, because Europe has no grace period. The university's deadline to claim rights under the Finnish university inventions act should also be checked [advisor note].
- **Funding so far:** Business Finland Research to Business pre-study (ended 2026) and an Academy of Finland project. The terms of both should be checked for any IP conditions.

## Assessment
**Main strengths**
- It tackles a clear process and regulatory pain point in a large, growing market.
- The raw material is cheap and widely available, and it has a domestic supply partner.
- Early performance matches or beats PVDF on a demanding cathode chemistry.
- Nothing has been published yet, so patent protection is still possible.

**Main risks**
- Lab results come from coin cells only and may not hold at full scale. The amount of data behind the comparison is Not stated.
- Water-based processing of NMC811 has known chemistry problems (see the advisor note under Benefits per costs).
- Battery-maker interest is unproven, and qualification will be slow.
- Competition from other water-based binders and from dry-electrode processing.
- There is no commercial lead, and the professor will not take part in a spin-off.
- The patent filing has to be timed around the spring 2027 manuscript.

**Questions to ask the researchers**
1. How many cells were tested, how much did the results vary, and how does the lignin binder perform at higher C-rates, higher temperatures and in pouch cells? Have you tried other cathodes such as LFP and other NMC grades?
2. How do you deal with lithium leaching, slurry pH and aluminium corrosion when NMC811 is processed in water? What is the moisture level in the dried electrode?
3. What does the chemical modification involve? What does it cost, and can it be scaled from 50 g to kilograms or tonnes? Can you put numbers on the cost and energy savings compared with PVDF/NMP?
4. Have the method or results been shared outside the group, for example at conferences, in theses or in Business Finland reports? Do the funders' terms or the pulp company's involvement affect who owns the IP?
5. What role does the postdoc want: a spin-off founder or the technical lead in a licensing deal? Would they work with an experienced business co-founder, and which battery or binder companies should be approached first?
